# Inflation, Purchasing Power, and Nominal vs. Real Returns

Inflation is the gradual, systemic increase in prices of goods and services over time across an entire economy. As inflation rises, each unit of currency buys a smaller percentage of a good or service, eroding purchasing power.

## Measuring Inflation: The Consumer Price Index (CPI)
Economists monitor inflation predominantly through the Consumer Price Index (CPI), calculated by statistical agencies (such as the US Bureau of Labor Statistics). The CPI tracks price changes for a standardized market basket of thousands of goods and services commonly purchased by urban households, encompassing food, shelter, energy, apparel, medical care, and transportation.

## The Mathematical Relationship: Nominal vs. Real Return
When evaluating investment performance, it is vital to differentiate between nominal returns and real purchasing power returns:
- Nominal Return: The raw percentage gain on an investment before accounting for inflation.
- Real Return: The actual gain in purchasing power after subtracting inflation.

The Fisher Equation expresses this relationship:
(1 + r_nominal) = (1 + r_real) * (1 + i)
A widely utilized close approximation is:
Real Return ≈ Nominal Return - Inflation Rate

Example:
If your savings account or certificate of deposit pays 4.0% nominal interest, but the annual inflation rate is 3.5%, your real economic return is approximately only 0.5%. If taxes are owed on the nominal interest, your post-tax real return may even be negative, meaning your cash is actively losing purchasing power despite nominally growing in balance.

## Assets That Historically Hedge Against Inflation
To protect long-term wealth from inflation risk, investors hold assets whose earnings or underlying asset values tend to adjust upward alongside consumer prices:
1. Broad Equities (Stocks): Productive businesses can raise prices on consumers to preserve profit margins, resulting in dividend growth and share price appreciation exceeding historical inflation over multi-decade horizons.
2. Real Estate: Physical properties offer intrinsic utility; landlords can raise rental rates during inflationary cycles, and replacement construction costs rise.
3. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS): Sovereign bonds whose principal value automatically adjusts upward with changes in the official Consumer Price Index.
4. Commodities and Natural Resources: Raw materials (energy, industrial metals, agriculture) often constitute the core inputs that drive cost-push inflation.
